title: Contract WS01 · Contract formation
recap:
- Shop displays and ordinary adverts are invitations to treat, so the customer makes the offer; only a unilateral advert, like a reward, is an offer.
- A reply that changes the terms is a counter-offer and destroys the offer; a mere question leaves it open.
- A posted acceptance takes effect on posting, unless the offer requires actual receipt, post is unreasonable or the letter is misaddressed; a revocation takes effect only on receipt.
- An offer can be revoked any time before acceptance, even if promised open, unless the offeree gave consideration or has started to perform a unilateral offer; the performer must have known of the offer, and motive does not matter.
- A vague key term, or an essential term left to be agreed with no machinery, means no contract; a meaningless subsidiary term is severed, and silence on price gets a reasonable price.
- A lock-out binds only if it is for a definite period and supported by consideration; a promise to negotiate in good faith is unenforceable.

## Rule
Will (warm): Right. You're walking past a shop. There's a scarf in the window with £12 on the label. You march in, point at it and say, "I'll have that for £12." Does the shop have to sell it to you?
Hannah (confident): Yes. It's got the price on it. That's basically a promise.
Will (amused): <short pause> No.
Hannah (incredulous): What? It says £12 right there!
Will: I know. But goods on display in a shop, or in the shop window, are an invitation to treat. The shop isn't offering anything. It's inviting you to make the offer.
Hannah (thinking aloud): So I'm the one offering. Me, standing there, holding a scarf like an idiot.
Will (laughing): At the till, yes. You offer, and the shop accepts or refuses. That's the Boots case. <laugh>
Hannah: So "the shop must sell at the label price" is the tempting answer, and it's wrong.
Will: Ruled out, yes. Ordinary adverts are the same, by the way. Invitations to treat. An invitation to treat only invites offers, so whoever responds is the one making the offer.
Hannah (wry): And in the scarf shop, that's me again.
Will (brisk): Let me give you the whole map, because everything hangs off it. You need an offer, a real one, not an invitation to treat.
Hannah: And then it just has to be accepted.
Will: Not so fast. Check the offer's still open: not rejected, not lapsed, not revoked. Then test the acceptance. Does it mirror the offer, was the method right, was it communicated, and when did it take effect?
Hannah (dry): That's a lot of questions for one yes.
Will: It is. And at the very end, are the agreed terms certain enough to enforce?
Hannah: So an offer, still alive, an acceptance, and terms that actually mean something.
Will: That's it. Each question turns on one point in that chain, and the trick is spotting which. Start with the offer, then. An offer is a statement of willingness to be bound on definite terms as soon as it's accepted.
Hannah: As soon as it's accepted. So the moment I say yes—
Will: I'm bound, yes. And whether I showed that willingness is judged objectively. What would a reasonable person in the offeree's position understand? My private intentions don't count.
Hannah (teasing): So "I didn't really mean it" gets you nowhere.
Will: Not if it's only in my head, no. And hedged wording isn't an offer either. "May be prepared to sell" is not an offer.
Hannah (amused): "May be prepared to" is the language of someone who really doesn't want to commit. Okay. But hang on. Is every advert just an invitation to treat? Because I've definitely seen adverts that say, "do this and we'll pay you."
Will (pleased): Good, that's the exception. An advert is an offer only if it's unilateral. Clear terms, showing an intention to be bound by performance. A reward, say, or a sum of money deposited to show they mean it.
Hannah (guessing): So any advert with clear terms.
Will: Close, but no. Clear terms alone aren't enough. It's a promise to pay anyone who does a stated act, with the intention to be bound shown. That's Carlill, the smoke ball case.
Hannah: Right. A normal advert says "come and buy this". A unilateral one says "do this and we pay you, and we mean it."
Will: That's your look-alike, advert as invitation to treat against advert as unilateral offer. And a unilateral offer is accepted by completing the performance. No notice of acceptance needed.
Hannah: So I don't ring up and say, "I'm on it"?
Will: Nobody needs telling. You do the thing, you've accepted.
Hannah (sceptical): The whole thing, though. Not most of it.
Will: Completing performance, yes. We'll come back to what happens if somebody yanks the offer halfway through. That's one of the nasty bits.
Hannah (guessing): What about auctions? The auctioneer's putting the thing up, so he's the one offering. I accept by bidding.
Will: Other way round. The bid is the offer. The auctioneer accepts on the fall of the hammer.
Hannah: So until the hammer comes down, I can change my mind.
Will: You can withdraw before the hammer falls, yes. Or before completion is announced in another customary manner. That's the Sale of Goods Act. Now, "without reserve". You're the highest genuine bidder and the auctioneer simply refuses to sell to you.
Hannah (indignant): Then I get the goods. He can't just say no.
Will (wry): <short pause> That's the trap. You don't get the goods. The hammer never fell, so there's no sale. You have a claim for damages against the auctioneer, on a collateral contract.
Hannah: So it's not the owner on the hook for the sale. Because there isn't one.
Will: No sale to be on the hook for. The claim is on the auctioneer's collateral promise.
Will (brisk): Tenders next. An invitation to tender is normally an invitation to treat, and each tender is an offer.
Hannah: Same as the shop. The inviter's just asking for offers.
Will: Same idea, with a twist. Say the tenders are solicited from a defined group, with a clear procedure. Then the inviter impliedly promises to consider every conforming tender submitted on time.
Hannah: Consider. Not accept.
Will (pleased): Not accept. It doesn't promise to accept one. That's Blackpool Aero Club. A conforming tender, in on time, has to be considered. Nobody has to pick it.
Hannah (wary): "On time" is doing a lot of work there.
Will: It's one of your numbers. On time, from a defined group, under a clear procedure, and the promise is to consider.
Hannah: And if they say they'll take the lowest bid?
Will: Different promise. An undertaking to accept the highest, or the lowest, conforming bid is a unilateral offer, accepted by submitting that bid.
Hannah (dry): "We'll consider it" and "we'll take the lowest" really are two different animals.
Will: Next stop on the map: is the offer still open? A rejection ends it, once it's communicated. It can't be accepted afterwards unless the offeror renews it.
Hannah: So I say no thanks, wake up the next day, think better of it—
Will: Too late. Unless the offeror puts it on the table again.
Hannah (groaning): Harsh.
Will: Time is the next killer. If the offer states a time limit, it lapses when that time expires, and a later acceptance makes no contract. If it states no time limit, it lapses after a reasonable time, judged by its subject matter. Either way the clock starts when the offer is made.
Hannah: Give me a number for reasonable.
Will: It depends on the subject matter. The example to remember is shares: five months was too long.
Hannah (amused): Five months. So no sitting on an offer of shares until you feel brave.
Will (laughing): It'll have lapsed by then, yes. Next, failure of a condition. If the offer was subject to a condition and the condition fails, the offer ends.
Hannah: Fine. And the offeror dying, I suppose.
Will: Yes. The offeror's death usually ends the offer only once the offeree knows of it, or where the contract is personal.
Hannah (dry): Cheerful workshop.
Will: And the last way an offer ends is revocation. That gets its own bit, because that's where the traps are.
Will (brisk): Then acceptance. It has to be an unqualified agreement to all the terms. That's the mirror image rule. And it has to be made with knowledge of the offer, in response to it.
Hannah: Okay. So I say, "Yes, I'll buy your bike, but only if you throw in a helmet."
Will (amused): Go on. What have you done?
Hannah (guessing): Accepted. It's a yes with a little extra.
Will: No. A reply that adds or changes terms is a counter-offer. It rejects and destroys the original offer, and the original can't be accepted later. Hyde and Wrench.
Hannah (groaning): Destroys. Not "pauses".
Will: Destroys. "Yes, but" is really "no, how about this instead".
Hannah (wary): And if I only ask a question? Can delivery dates vary, say?
Will: That's fine. A mere request for information isn't a counter-offer. The offer stays open.
Hannah: So the test is whether I changed the terms or just asked about them.
Will: Yes. Counter-offer against request for information is a look-alike pair. And the trap is treating any reply as a counter-offer. It's ruled out where the reply only asks a question about the terms.
Hannah: And if a counter-offer does get accepted?
Will: Then its terms become the contract's terms.
Will (dry): Which brings us to the battle of the forms. Two businesses, each firing their own standard terms at the other.
Hannah (thinking aloud): So every new form is a counter-offer. Which kills the one before.
Will: Each new form is a counter-offer, yes. And the contract is usually on the last form sent before the other side accepts, by words or by conduct. Delivering the goods, say, or taking them. Butler Machine Tool and Ex-Cell-O. The last shot.
Hannah (confident): Hang on. First shot wins. Whoever sent the first form was the offeror, so the terms are theirs.
Will: That's the other half of the pair, and no. Last shot wins. The first form was destroyed by the second.
Hannah (pleased): Because the second one was a counter-offer. Got it.
Will: And there's a tail on it. If the parties perform but neither side's terms were ever accepted, a contract on implied terms may still arise.
Hannah: So nobody's terms win, and there's still a deal.
Will: May still arise. No more than that. Silence next, and silence is not acceptance, even if the offeror says it will be.
Hannah: So I can't write, "if I don't hear back, you've agreed."
Will: You can write it. It does nothing. An offeror can't impose a contract by silence. Felthouse and Bindley.
Hannah (dry): Well, that's all of my inbox saved.
Will: But doing something can be acceptance. Acceptance by conduct is acting in a way that objectively shows acceptance of the offered terms. Trading on an unsigned draft, say.
Hannah: So silence is nothing, but positive performance on the offered terms is a yes.
Will: Those are the two to keep apart. Silence versus acceptance by conduct.
Will: That "in response to it" bit has a consequence. Identical cross-offers make no contract. Neither is an acceptance, because an acceptance has to respond to a known offer.
Hannah (incredulous): Two letters crossing in the post, same thing on the same terms, and nothing?
Will: Nothing. You both want it, but neither of you was accepting anything.
Hannah: Rotten luck.
Will: Method next. If the offeror prescribes a method, another method that's no less advantageous to the offeror still binds, unless the offeror made clear that only that method will do.
Hannah: So if they say "reply by letter" and I hand them the letter, I'm fine.
Will: If that's no less advantageous to them, yes. Unless the offer made clear that only a posted letter will do.
Hannah (teasing): Control freaks.
Will (laughing): Exclusive control freaks. Those are the exception.
Will (brisk): Then communication. Acceptance has to be communicated, which means actually received by the offeror. Entores and Miles Far East.
Hannah: So it has to arrive.
Will: That's the starting point. Two things let you off it. The postal rule, and a unilateral offer, where completed performance does the job.
Hannah: The famous lost letter.
Will: Where use of the post is reasonable, a letter of acceptance takes effect when it's properly posted, even if it's—
Hannah (incredulous): <gasp> Lost? The offeror never hears a thing, and there's a contract?
Will (amused): <short pause> Delayed or lost. It's made the moment the letter's properly posted. Adams and Lindsell.
Hannah: And properly posted means what? I can't just toss it over the neighbour's hedge.
Will: Correctly addressed and stamped. And put in an official post box, or handed to a Post Office employee authorised to receive letters.
Hannah: What about giving it to the postman when he turns up at my door?
Will: Not if he's delivery-only. Handing it to a delivery-only postman doesn't count.
Hannah (thoughtful): Because he isn't there to receive letters. Fine.
Will: Now, "where use of the post is reasonable". The postal rule can be lost three ways. Have a go at one.
Hannah (guessing): The offer says no.
Will: Yes. The offer expressly or impliedly excludes it. "Notice in writing to" the offeror means actual receipt. Holwell Securities and Hughes. Another?
Hannah (thinking aloud): Post's just a daft way to reply. If the offer's urgent, say.
Will (pleased): Where use of the post is unreasonable, for example where the offer implies a prompt reply. One more.
Hannah: I've got nothing.
Will: The offeree misaddresses the letter.
Hannah (dry): So the one job I had, writing the address, and I've lost the whole rule.
Will: In all three the postal rule's gone, so the normal rule is back. The acceptance has to be received. And that's your trap: a lost letter still binds. It's ruled out where the wording requires the acceptance to reach the offeror.
Hannah: Right. And if I ring you up to accept instead?
Will: Phone, telex, fax, email. Instantaneous methods all take effect when and where they're received by the offeror. Entores again.
Hannah: So postal rule versus receipt rule is just the method.
Will: Yes. Post versus phone, telex, fax or email.
Hannah (wary): And an email that lands at night, when nobody's in?
Will: Received outside business hours. It's generally effective when the office next opens, but it depends on intentions, business practice and risk.
Hannah (wry): "It depends." My favourite thing to hear in a law lecture.
Will: There's a default in it, though: generally the office next opening. The depends part is intentions, business practice and risk.
Will: One more bit on communication. If a third party passes the acceptance on, it binds only if the offeree authorised that person. Powell and Lee.
Hannah: So my mate can't ring you up and say, "oh, she's definitely in", unless I asked him to.
Will: Not unless you authorised him, no. Keep that one in your pocket, because revocation has a look-alike rule, and it goes the other way.
Will: Right, the loose end from earlier. Revocation. An offer can be revoked at any time before acceptance, even if the offeror promised to keep it open. Routledge and Grant.
Hannah (indignant): Even if they promised? A promise is a promise.
Will: Unless the offeree gave consideration for it. That's an option, and then the offer can't just be pulled.
Hannah: And if I gave nothing?
Will (dry): Then it doesn't bind. The tempting answer is that a promise to keep the offer open binds, and it's ruled out where the offeree gave nothing for that promise.
Hannah: So bare promise versus option contract, and it all comes down to what the offeree gave.
Will: Consideration, yes. Or the promise was made by deed.
Hannah: Okay. So revoking is easy. I just say the offer's off.
Will: You say it, and it has to be received, before the acceptance. Byrne and Van Tienhoven. The postal rule never applies to revocation.
Hannah (confident): So whoever posts first wins. Post your revocation before I post my acceptance, and you're out.
Will: That's the tempting answer, and no. Posting a revocation does nothing. It takes effect only on receipt. If your acceptance was posted before my revocation arrived, the acceptance binds.
Hannah (groaning): <sigh> What a lopsided rule.
Will: Isn't it? Post helps the person accepting and never the person revoking. The postal rule covers acceptances only.
Hannah: So timing, in one go. Post takes effect on posting. Phone and email on receipt. Revocation always on receipt.
Will: Always.
Will (thoughtful): Now the mate rule. A revocation can reach you through a third party. A reliable one will do, it doesn't have to come from the offeror. Dickinson and Dodds.
Hannah (sceptical): So some bloke in the pub says "the offer's off" and that's that?
Will: Reliable, not some bloke. And that's the trap. You might think it's ineffective because the third party was unauthorised. It isn't. A revocation needs only a reliable source. An acceptance passed on by a third party needs the offeree's authority.
Hannah: Acceptance through a mate needs authority. Revocation through a mate needs the mate to be reliable.
Will: That's the pair.
Hannah (guessing): If an offer went out to everybody, I'd guess you revoke it the way you made it.
Will: Yes. A unilateral offer to the world is revoked by taking the same steps as were used to make it.
Hannah: Same steps in, same steps out.
Will: Now the big exception. Revocation before acceptance versus a unilateral offer once performance has begun. If the offeree has started to perform, the offer can't be revoked. The offeree must be allowed to complete. That's Errington and Woods.
Hannah (thinking aloud): So say I promise to pay you for painting my whole fence. You're halfway along and I ring up and say forget it.
Will: Too late. I've started, so I must be allowed to finish. Before I'd started, you could have revoked it.
Hannah: And before you've started, it's the ordinary revocation rule.
Will: Yes. Whether the offeree has started to perform a unilateral offer, that's the whole difference.
Will (brisk): And one last thing on unilateral offers, because the exam loves it. The performer must have known of the offer when acting. Motive is otherwise irrelevant.
Hannah (guessing): So you find a lost cat, you bring it back because you love cats, you don't care about the reward, and you've got no claim.
Will: That's the tempting wrong answer. Knowledge decides, not motive. If you knew about the reward when you acted, your reasons don't matter. If you'd never seen it, you've got no claim.
Hannah: Knew and acted for other reasons: claim. Never knew: nothing.
Will: Right. It all turns on whether the performer knew of the offer when acting.
Will (brisk): And last on the map, certainty. Are the agreed terms certain enough to enforce?
Hannah: I thought an offer and an acceptance was the end of it.
Will: Not if the terms are mush. If a key term is too vague for the court to give it a meaning, there's no contract. "On hire-purchase terms" was too vague, because hire-purchase terms varied widely. Scammell and Ouston.
Hannah (groaning): Which hire-purchase terms? There's nothing to pin it down.
Will: Quite. Next, an agreement to agree an essential term in the future. Price "to be agreed", say. That's not binding.
Hannah: Unless—?
Will: Unless there's machinery in the contract to fix it. Arbitration, for instance. Then the agreement is binding.
Hannah (pleased): So "price to be agreed" with a way to settle it is fine. "Price to be agreed" with nothing is dead.
Will: With no machinery, it's not binding. Yes.
Hannah (wary): And if the contract just says nothing about price?
Will: Different. A sale contract that's silent on price gets a reasonable price implied. Sale of Goods Act.
Hannah (sceptical): That feels like the same problem.
Will: It isn't, and that's the trap. It's price left "to be agreed" with no machinery that fails. Silent on price is fine.
Hannah: Silent is fine. "To be agreed" with nothing behind it is the killer.
Will: And a meaningless subsidiary term can just be ignored. Severed, and the rest stays enforceable.
Hannah: So one nonsense phrase doesn't sink the deal.
Will: Not if it's subsidiary. Only a vague key term defeats the contract. That's the look-alike: vague key term, no contract. Meaningless subsidiary term, severed. Is the term key, or merely subsidiary?
Hannah (guessing): And the big tempting answer is "the whole deal fails".
Will: Which is ruled out three ways. The bad term is a meaningless subsidiary one. Or the contract is silent on price. Or machinery fixes the term.
Hannah: Subsidiary, silent, machinery. Three escapes.
Will: Another help. Courts are more willing to find a contract certain where it's been performed than where it's wholly executory.
Hannah: Executory?
Will: The opposite of performed.
Hannah: So if everyone's already got on and done it, the court is more ready to say it's certain.
Will: And the court may even imply a term to complete an agreement the parties intended to be binding, rather than find no contract.
Hannah (thoughtful): May patch it, if they meant it to bind.
Will: That's it. Now promises about negotiating. An agreement to negotiate in good faith is unenforceable. Walford and Miles.
Hannah (wry): Which sounds like the nicest promise there is.
Will: And the least enforceable. A lock-out is different. That's a promise not to negotiate with others. It's enforceable only if it's for a definite period and supported by consideration. Pitt and PHH Asset Management.
Hannah: Both needed. Definite period and consideration.
Will: Both. Take away either and it isn't enforceable.
Hannah: And "while negotiations continue" is no definite period at all.
Will: Right. An indefinite lock-out like that, or a lock-in, is uncertain and unenforceable.
Hannah (dry): I'll file lock-in under same fate.
Will: Same fate.
## Exam mode
Will (brisk): Right, exam hat on. Nadia emails Gareth an offer to sell him her allotment shed. The email says acceptance must be received by her by 5pm Friday. On Thursday, Gareth posts a correctly addressed, stamped acceptance. It arrives on Monday. Is there a contract?
Hannah (confident): Yes. Postal rule. Posted Thursday, contract made Thursday. Correctly addressed, stamped, the lot.
Will: That's the bait in the question.
Hannah (groaning): Why?
Will: <short pause> The offer required acceptance to be received by Nadia. That excludes the postal rule, so posting wasn't enough. And nothing reached her before the offer lapsed at 5pm Friday.
Hannah (thinking aloud): So there's a stated time, the time ran out, and Monday's too late.
Will: No contract. "Must be received by me" is the sentence you hunt for.
Hannah: Right. Next.
Will: Marcus pays Elsie £1,000. In return, Elsie promises that for 21 days she won't talk to any other buyer about her pottery studio, and that both of them will "negotiate in good faith" towards a sale. On day 12, Elsie sells the studio to Hamza. Can Marcus sue Elsie, and on which promise?
Hannah (wary): I'd say no. It's an agreement to negotiate, and those are unenforceable.
Will: Tempting. Try splitting the two promises.
Hannah (thinking aloud): Okay. Good faith, unenforceable, fine. But "won't talk to anyone else for 21 days" is a lock-out. A definite period, 21 days, and he paid £1,000, so there's consideration. And day 12 is well inside it.
Will (pleased): So Marcus can sue Elsie for breach of the—
Hannah: —lock-out promise. The good-faith one is unenforceable and adds nothing.
Will: And the tempting answer lumped the two together. A lock-out is a negative promise with a fixed end, so it survives.
Hannah (teasing): Another? I can take it.
Will (brisk): Two quick ones now. A toaster sits on a shop shelf marked £25. A customer takes it to the till and says, "I'll pay £25." The shop says no. Must it sell?

Hannah (dry): Even though the cat's home either way.

Hannah (guessing): Counter-offer. He asked about delivery, so he's added a term and killed the offer.

Hannah (catching on): Asked. It's a question, so the offer stays open and "I'll take it at £800" is a proper acceptance. Contract.

Hannah (wry): Counter-offer, this time. He changed the price, which destroyed the original offer, so his "fine" has nothing left to accept. No contract.

Will: Ruth writes offering Callum her record player. She changes her mind and posts a revocation first thing in the morning. Post is a reasonable way to reply and nothing in the offer says otherwise. Contract?

Will: Suppose one phrase is meaningless but it's only a subsidiary term, and the rest is clear.
Hannah: Sever it. The rest stands. Only a vague key term sinks it.
Will: And if the vague one is a key term, like "on hire-purchase terms" when those terms varied widely?
Hannah: No contract.
Will: Last tweak. Both sides have already been performing it.
Hannah: Then the court is more ready to find it certain.
Will (brisk): Quick-fire. An offeror writes, "silence means yes." The offeree says nothing.
Hannah: No acceptance. Silence isn't acceptance, even if the offeror says it will be.
Will: The other side starts trading on the unsigned draft.
Hannah: Acceptance by conduct.
Will: An acceptance emailed late at night, with the office shut.
Hannah: Instantaneous, so the receipt rule. Generally effective when the office next opens, but it depends on intentions, business practice and risk.
Will: An offer of shares with no time limit, accepted five months later.
Hannah: Lapsed. Five months was too long for shares.
Will: Two letters cross, same offer, same terms, each unaware of the other.
Hannah: No contract. Neither responds to a known offer.
Will: The offer says reply by letter. The offeree hand-delivers a signed acceptance, which is no less advantageous to the offeror.
Hannah: Binds, unless the offer made clear only a posted letter would do.
Will: A bidder changes his mind before the hammer falls.
Hannah: He can withdraw.
## Recap
Will (warm): Right. Quick lap of honour. Shop window first. Display goods and ordinary adverts are invitations to treat, so you, the customer, make the offer. Only a unilateral advert, a reward, say, is itself an offer.
Hannah: A reply that changes the terms is a counter-offer, and the original offer is destroyed. Just asking a question? The offer stays open.
Will: Post. Acceptance takes effect on posting. Unless the offer requires actual receipt, or post is unreasonable, or you misaddress the letter.
Hannah (wry): And revocation only on receipt. Always. Post never helps the person revoking.
Will: You can revoke any time before acceptance, even if you promised to keep it open. Unless the offeree gave consideration, or has started to perform a unilateral offer.
Hannah: And for a unilateral offer you must have known of it. Motive doesn't matter.
Will (brisk): Certainty. A vague key term, or an essential term left to be agreed with no machinery, means no contract. But a meaningless subsidiary term gets severed, and silence on price gets a reasonable price.
Hannah: Last one. A lock-out binds only if it's for a definite period and supported by consideration. A promise to negotiate in good faith is unenforceable.
Will (pleased): Lovely. Go and buy a scarf.
