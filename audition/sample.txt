## Map
Will (brisk): Right, evidence of fact: what witnesses can put before the court. The experts get their own episode. Five topics. What gets in at all. How witness statements are served and used. What a statement looks like, and when you need an affidavit.
Hannah: So: what gets in, statements in use, statements in form.
Will: Yes. Then hearsay. And last, summonses and how the trial runs.
Hannah (pleased): Then hearsay, then summonses and trial. Five topics. I can hold that.
## Block 1: Evidence basics, admissibility and opinion evidence
Will: Right: five topics. Evidence basics are the gate: what gets in at all. The first test is dull, but everything hangs from it. Evidence has to be relevant to a fact in issue.
Hannah (dry): And a fact in issue is whatever the lawyers are arguing about.
Will: Close. The statements of case define the facts in issue. And evidence is relevant if it tends to make a fact in issue more probable or less probable. Irrelevant evidence is inadmissible. That's the why for the whole block: the statements of case mark out what the fight is about, so the evidence has to be about that fight.
Hannah: Fine. So relevant means it gets in?
Will: Not automatically. Relevant evidence can still be withheld or excluded, by three routes. One, privilege. Two, without prejudice communications. Three, the court's control.
Hannah: Court's control sounds like the vague one.
Will: It's wide. The court can direct three things. One, which issues need evidence. Two, what kind of evidence is needed. Three, how it's placed before the court.
Hannah: And can it keep evidence out?
Will: Yes. It can exclude evidence that would otherwise be admissible, and it can limit cross-examination. Think of twelve witnesses on a minor point: the judge can limit the cross-examination.
Hannah: And without prejudice?
Will: A without prejudice offer is not put before the judge, however relevant it is.
Hannah (thinking aloud): Right. So what if someone got the evidence badly? They filmed the other side without telling them.
Will: This is a starred point, and it's a trap. Improperly obtained evidence, covert surveillance for instance, is generally admissible in civil cases. The court may exclude it, or it may reflect the conduct in costs.
Hannah (surprised): So being sneaky doesn't keep it out.
Will: Right, not by itself. <short pause> The trap wording is "not excluded for being improperly obtained".
Hannah: What about us? Can a solicitor tidy up the evidence?
Will: No: a solicitor must not misuse or tamper with evidence, so no altering or destroying documents, and must not seek to influence its substance. Two notices come next.
Hannah: Go on.
Will: A notice to admit facts has to be served no later than 21 days before trial. If a fact isn't admitted and it's later proved, the refusing party may pay the costs of proving it.
Hannah: And the document one?
Will: A disclosed document's authenticity is deemed admitted unless a notice to prove it is served by the latest date for witness statements, or within 7 days of disclosure, whichever is later.
Hannah: So if nobody serves anything, the document is treated as genuine.
Will: Yes: with no notice, authenticity is deemed admitted. Last part of the block: opinion evidence. Witnesses give evidence of fact, not opinion. Opinion is admissible in two situations only: from an expert on a matter requiring expertise, or from a lay witness as a way of conveying facts they personally perceived. Think speed, distance, or that someone seemed drunk.
Hannah: Where does a treating doctor fit?
Will: That's the look-alike. A professional who treated or dealt with the claimant, a treating doctor for example, usually gives evidence of fact about what they did and saw. Opinion evidence from them needs permission, as expert evidence.
Hannah (summing up): Evidence basics, then. Relevance first, and relevant evidence can still be excluded. Improperly obtained evidence generally goes in. Then two notices, and opinion only from experts or as perceived fact.
Will: Right: relevance, the ways in which relevant evidence can still fall out, and who may give opinion. That's what gets in. Next, how a witness's evidence actually arrives: the witness statement.
## Block 2: Witness statements: use, exchange and time limits
Will: Witness statements. Served statements show each side the other's factual evidence, and at trial they normally stand as evidence in chief. The exam questions turn on the awkward cases: late, unused or unobtainable statements.
Hannah: Start with what a witness statement is.
Will: A written statement, signed by a person, of the evidence that person could give orally. The court orders each party to serve statements of the oral evidence of fact it will rely on at trial, usually by simultaneous exchange.
Hannah: And at trial the witness gets up and tells it all again?
Will: No, and that's the trap. The served statement stands as evidence in chief, unless the court orders otherwise. It isn't oral evidence in chief.
Hannah: What if the witness remembers something new on the day?
Will: Amplifying the statement, or adding new matters arising since service, needs permission, and there has to be good reason.
Hannah: Can the other side cross-examine on it?
Will: Yes. A witness who's called can be cross-examined on the statement, whether or not it was referred to in chief. And the court shapes all this. It can limit the issues for factual evidence, identify the witnesses who are called or read, and limit the length or format of statements.
Hannah: Right, so what are the awkward cases?
Will: Three, all starred. One: the statement isn't served in time. Then the witness gives oral evidence only with permission, and the court applies relief from sanctions principles.
Hannah: So late means permission, and it's not a formality. Next?
Will: Yes, and the permission isn't a formality. Two: the statement was served, but the witness is neither called nor is the statement put in as hearsay by the party who served it. Then any other party may put it in as hearsay.
Hannah (amused): So you serve it, drop the witness, and the other side can still put your statement in.
Will: Yes: your own statement can be used against you, as hearsay, by anyone else.
Hannah: Three?
Will: Three: no statement is obtainable at all. Then you apply without notice for permission to serve a witness summary. The summary gives the evidence if it's known, or otherwise the matters the witness will be asked about.
Hannah: What else goes in a summary?
Will: The witness's name and address, unless the court orders otherwise, and it's served within the time for witness statements. And a witness summons may also be needed, because a summary doesn't make anybody turn up.
Hannah: Give me a picture.
Will: Say a former site manager saw a scaffold give way, and now works for the other side's subcontractor and flatly refuses to sign anything for you. You can't serve a witness statement, because a statement has to be signed by the witness. So you apply for permission to serve a summary, and you get a summons to secure his attendance.
Hannah: And once there is a statement, does it need anything?
Will: Now the last part of the block: what the statement needs, and where it can travel. With no statement of truth, the statement may be held inadmissible. And a false statement made without honest belief in its truth can lead to contempt proceedings, which need the court's permission.
Hannah: Can I take someone's witness statement and use it for something else?
Will: That's collateral use. A witness statement can be used only for these proceedings, with three exceptions. One, the witness consents in writing. Two, the court permits. Three, it was put in evidence at a public hearing.
Hannah (wary): That sounds a lot like the rule for disclosed documents.
Will: It's the look-alike. For a disclosed document it's also a public hearing or permission, but the third route is the agreement of the disclosing party and the owner of the document.
Hannah: So the question is whose consent. The witness for a statement, the disclosing party and owner for a document.
Will: Yes. Whose consent is the whole difference.
Hannah (summing up): Witness statements: a served statement stands as evidence in chief, and late, orphaned and unobtainable each have their own way through.
Will: Right: late needs permission, unused can be put in by the other side, and unobtainable needs permission for a summary. Next, what a statement has to look like, and the sworn alternative.
## Block 3: Form of witness statements; affidavits
Will: Form of witness statements, and affidavits. A witness statement should be in the witness's own words where practicable, and it's verified by a statement of truth the witness signs. The why: at trial the served statement normally stands as the witness's evidence in chief, so it has to be the witness's own account. An affidavit is the sworn form, needed only where something requires it.
Hannah: So one default, one special.
Will: Yes: the statement is the default, the affidavit the special case. The statement first. If practicable it's in the witness's own words, their own language, and the first person.
Hannah: And what does it say about the witness?
Will: It gives the witness's identity details and says how it was prepared, such as face to face, by telephone or through an interpreter.
Hannah: That's a lot of boxes.
Will: Mostly look-up. The parts worth keeping: it distinguishes what the witness knows personally from information and belief, and gives the source. It uses numbered paragraphs and identifies exhibits.
Hannah: What if the witness doesn't speak English?
Will: Then it's drafted in their language, translated, and the foreign-language statement is filed. The translator signs the original and certifies accuracy.
Hannah: And who signs the statement of truth?
Will: Starred. The witness, not the legal representative. And the wording includes a warning that contempt proceedings may be brought against anyone who makes a false statement without an honest belief in its truth.
Hannah (thinking aloud): But a solicitor can sign a statement of truth on a statement of case...
Will: Yes, on a statement of case. Never on a witness statement. That's the trap: not the solicitor's signature.
Hannah: Anything extra for the big courts?
Will: Business and Property Courts trial statements. Only facts within personal knowledge that are relevant to the issues, in the witness's own words. No argument, and no commentary on documents.
Hannah: Anything else those statements need?
Will: Three more things. One, a list of documents referred to. Two, the witness's confirmation. Three, the legal representative's certificate of compliance. Breach can bring sanctions.
Hannah: Now the sworn one.
Will: An affidavit is written evidence sworn on oath or affirmed before an authorised person, for example a solicitor, a commissioner for oaths or a court officer. It's required only where something requires it, and there are four sources.
Hannah: Which four?
Will: One, the court. Two, a rule. Three, a practice direction. Four, another enactment. The affidavit examples are freezing injunctions, search orders and contempt applications. Otherwise, use a witness statement.
Hannah: And who can swear me in?
Will: Someone independent. Not the deponent's own solicitor, and not a member or employee of the acting firm. Say it's an urgent freezing injunction and a trainee at your own firm offers to take the oath to save time. The answer is no, because the trainee is an employee of the acting firm.
Hannah (mock-outraged): <laugh> Saving time was the whole selling point.
Will: Next, a starred point. A voluntary affidavit at a non-trial hearing is allowed, but the extra cost is not recoverable from another party unless the court orders otherwise.
Hannah: So it isn't inadmissible. You just pay for the luxury.
Will: Right: allowed, but the extra cost is lost. That's the trap. Not inadmissible; only the cost, unless the court orders otherwise.
Hannah: And lying in one?
Will: A false statement in an affidavit is possible perjury, which is a crime, as well as contempt. The muddle is thinking an affidavit lie is only contempt.
Hannah: And in a witness statement?
Will: Contempt also lies against anyone who makes, or causes, a false statement in a statement of case or a witness statement verified by a statement of truth, without honest belief. And the second trap: a contempt application itself needs an affidavit or affirmation, not a witness statement.
Hannah: Is there anything about how an affidavit is set out?
Will: It's set out like a statement: heading, the deponent's details, first person and numbered paragraphs.
Hannah: And own knowledge kept apart?
Will: Yes, own knowledge distinguished from information and belief. Then exhibits are marked, the jurat is signed by the deponent and the person before whom it's sworn, and alterations are initialled.
Hannah: Let me put the two side by side. Witness statement: verified by a statement of truth the witness signs, nobody to swear it before, the default for trial and interim evidence, and a lie can lead to contempt proceedings, but only with the court's permission.
Will: And the affidavit?
Hannah: Oath or affirmation, an independent authorised person, only where required, and a lie can be perjury as well as contempt.
Will: Yes: the statement is the everyday form, and the affidavit is for where something requires it, with perjury as the extra risk.
Hannah (summing up): Form and affidavits: witness signs the statement of truth, affidavit only where required, sworn before someone independent.
Will: Right: the witness signs, and the affidavit is the exception. Now the one with the biggest exam traps: hearsay.
## Block 4: Hearsay and previous convictions
Will: Hearsay. Civil hearsay is admissible, and the court weighs it. The why: unlike the criminal rules, civil proceedings never exclude evidence just for being hearsay, so the questions turn on notice, the 14-day steps and weight. It's inadmissible only if, and to the extent, the maker is shown not to have been competent when the statement was made. And missing notice never makes it inadmissible.
Hannah (wary): I'll want to cross-check those three.
Will: Good, because the exam tests the gaps between them. First, what hearsay is. A statement made otherwise than by a person giving oral evidence in the proceedings, tendered as evidence of the matters stated.
Hannah: So the neighbour tells me the pipe burst, and I tell the court the neighbour said so, to prove the pipe burst.
Will: Yes, that's hearsay. It includes multiple hearsay too, such as a witness repeating what a bystander heard from someone else. And in civil proceedings, hearsay isn't excluded because it's hearsay.
Hannah: And if I only want to show the neighbour said it?
Will: Then it's original evidence, not hearsay. You're relying on it only to show it was said, not that it's true.
Hannah: So civil hearsay is just in. That seems strangely easy.
Will: Yes, and that's the look-alike trap. Criminal hearsay rules are exclusion, subject to exceptions. Civil proceedings never exclude it for being hearsay.
Hannah: Civil: admitted and weighed. Criminal: excluded unless an exception.
Will: Right, that's the whole contrast. Now the one thing that does exclude it. Starred. Hearsay is inadmissible if, and to the extent, it's shown the maker was not competent as a witness when it was made, through mental or physical infirmity or lack of understanding. And a child is treated as competent if they meet the Children Act test.
Hannah: "To the extent". So part of it can go and the rest stays.
Will: Yes: inadmissible to the extent the maker is shown not competent, and no further. Now notice, which is where the traps live. A hearsay notice is not needed in four cases. One, evidence at a hearing other than trial, such as an interim application or summary judgment. Two, a trial affidavit or witness statement containing no hearsay. Three, a probate statement alleged to be the deceased's. Four, where a practice direction excludes the requirement.
Hannah: So it's a trial, it's hearsay, and it's not one of those four.
Will: Right: then you do need to give notice. Starred. If the witness is called, serving the witness statement is the notice. If the maker isn't called, you serve the statement and say that the witness won't be called, and why.
Hannah: And if it's not in a witness statement?
Will: Otherwise you serve a hearsay notice. It does three things. One, identifies the evidence. Two, states the intention to rely on it. Three, says why the maker won't be called. The deadline is no later than the latest date for serving witness statements.
Hannah: Say I miss it.
Will: Starred, and it's the point of the whole block. With no notice, the evidence is still admissible. The court may reflect that in case management and costs, and it may give the evidence less weight.
Hannah: Show me the difference.
Will: Two little stories, because they tell you what decides it. Aisha relies at trial on her neighbour Dev's statement about the flood but never serves a hearsay notice. It's still admissible. The court may give it less weight and reflect the failure in costs. Now suppose Dev made the statement while he lacked understanding through mental infirmity, and that's shown. Then it's inadmissible to that extent, notice or no notice.
Hannah: So a missing notice goes to weight. An incompetent maker goes to admissibility.
Will: Right, that's it. Only the maker's incompetence when the statement was made excludes hearsay. Not inadmissible for want of notice is the trap line.
Hannah: One line: missing notice is weight, costs and case management; incompetent maker is out.
Will: Right: weight for the missing notice, admissibility for the maker. After notice, there are two 14-day steps, both from service of the hearsay notice. Starred: where the maker isn't called, any other party may apply for permission to call and cross-examine the maker, within 14 days after service of the notice.
Hannah (groaning): <sigh> Fourteen days. I keep wanting to say 28.
Will: That's the trap. 28 days is the time for written questions to an expert, which belongs with the experts, in the next episode. The second step: to attack the credibility of a maker who isn't called, for example with a fraud conviction, you give notice within 14 days after service of the hearsay notice.
Hannah: And how does the court weigh hearsay?
Will: Starred, six factors, in two lots of three. Lot one. One, was it reasonable and practicable to call the maker. Two, was the statement contemporaneous. Three, is it multiple hearsay.
Hannah: Reasonable to call, contemporaneous, multiple.
Will: Right. Lot two. Four, any motive to conceal or misrepresent. Five, whether it was edited, collaborative or made for a purpose. Six, any attempt to prevent proper evaluation.
Hannah: So motive, how it was made, and interference.
Will: Yes, that's all six. Try this one. Marisol is suing over a flood in a block of flats. At trial, her side wants to rely on a written account by a tenant called Kofi. They never served a hearsay notice and they don't plan to call him. Kofi is healthy and lives round the corner. He wrote the account long after the flood, and Marisol's solicitor edited it for him. The other side objects that without notice it is simply out.
Hannah: So, is Kofi's statement inadmissible for want of notice; admissible but of little weight, with costs possibly reflecting the failure; or admissible at full strength, as if nothing were missing?
Hannah: <think>
Hannah (thinking aloud): This is hearsay. Hearsay is admissible in civil cases, and nothing says Kofi lacked competence when he wrote it, so it's in. Missing notice never excludes. It goes to weight, case management and costs. Weight: easy to call him, written long after so not contemporaneous, and edited. So the middle one.
Will: Yes. The deciding fact is that the only thing that excludes it is incompetence when it was made, and there's none here. The notice failure goes to weight, costs and case management. Three of the weight factors point one way: calling Kofi was reasonable and practicable, it wasn't contemporaneous, and it was edited.
Hannah: What's left on hearsay?
Will: Three loose ends, all about things that aren't witness statements. One: plans, photographs and models that aren't in a witness statement, an expert report or a disclosed list. You give notice by the latest date for witness statements, so the other side can inspect, and otherwise they're not usable without permission. Two: records of a business or public authority are received without further proof if an officer's signed certificate is produced. Three: a subsisting UK conviction is admissible to prove the person committed the offence, where it's relevant to an issue.
Hannah (summing up): Hearsay: civil hearsay is in and weighed, it's out only to the extent the maker is shown not competent when it was made, and no notice hurts weight and costs but never admissibility.
Will: Right: in and weighed, out only for an incompetent maker, and notice never decides admissibility. Next, getting the witness into court, and what happens when they're there.
## Block 5: Witness summonses and trial procedure
Will: Witness summonses and trial procedure. A summons compels attendance and a served statement does not. The why: a statement only sets out what a witness would say; it can't make them come, so if you need to compel a witness to attend, you need a summons. And there are two 7-day rules: issue decides permission, service decides binding effect.
Hannah: Two sevens, two jobs. Go on.
Will: A witness summons is a court document requiring a witness to attend court to give evidence, to produce documents, or both. You can issue it without permission, unless one of three things is true. One, you're seeking it less than 7 days before trial. Two, it's for a date other than the trial date.
Hannah: And the third?
Will: Three, it's for a hearing other than the trial. Any one of the three means you need permission.
Hannah: That's issue. What about binding?
Will: Binding is separate. A summons binds only if it's served at least 7 days before the date the witness must attend, unless the court directs that later service binds. That's the look-alike: summons issue versus summons binding. The trap is thinking early issue makes it binding.
Hannah: Issue date is permission, service date is binding.
Will: Yes, that's the split. Two little stories. A County Court trial is on 20 June. The summons is served on the witness on 9 June with conduct money, and she stays away without good reason. She can be fined up to £1,000. Now the same summons, served on 16 June, only 4 days before attendance. It's not binding, unless the court directed that later service binds. The deciding fact is the date of service, not the date of issue.
Hannah: What's conduct money?
Will: On service, offer or pay conduct money: reasonable travel to and from court, plus compensation for loss of time. Service is by the court, unless the party says it will serve. If the court serves, the party deposits the conduct money with the court. And a summons is available for any witness, including an expert whose report has been served.
Hannah: And if they ignore it?
Will: Starred, in two parts. Failure without good reason to obey a properly served summons, where conduct money was paid or tendered, is in the County Court a fine of up to £1,000. In the High Court, disobedience is contempt, punishable by a fine or imprisonment.
Hannah: Can a witness fight it?
Will: The court may set aside or vary a summons, for example if the witness cannot give relevant evidence, or it's oppressive, or it's a fishing exercise for documents.
Hannah: And the statement they've already served, doesn't that do the same job?
Will: Starred, and it ties back to witness statements. A served witness statement does not compel attendance. If the witness doesn't come, the statement may be used only as hearsay, with reduced weight, unless the court orders otherwise.
Hannah: And if they genuinely can't come?
Will: Say they're ill or abroad. Then evidence can be by video link or by deposition before an examiner. A witness abroad may need a letter of request. One rule for solicitors here too. No benefit to a witness may depend on the nature of the evidence or the outcome. Reasonable expenses and loss of time may be paid.
Hannah: Now the trial itself.
Will: Claimant's opening, which is often short or dispensed with because the judge has pre-read. Then the claimant's witnesses: examination-in-chief, cross-examination, re-examination. The defendant's witnesses in the same way.
Hannah: And closing speeches, claimant first, since they started.
Will: <short pause> No, that's the trap. Defendant first, claimant last. Then judgment.
Hannah (laughing): <laugh> Fine, fair, caught.
Will: And that's the look-alike. In a criminal trial, the defence speaks last. In civil, the claimant has the last word.
Hannah: Civil, claimant last. Criminal, defence last.
Will: Right: the last word flips. Civil chief is usually brief, because the statement stands. Criminal chief is normally oral, or a video-recorded interview under a special measures direction. Then a submission of no case to answer. In civil proceedings the defendant is normally put to its election. The judge usually hears the submission only if the defendant calls no evidence. If it fails, the case is decided on the claimant's evidence alone.
Hannah: And criminal?
Will: Second look-alike. In a criminal trial, the defence may submit no case to answer and still call evidence if it fails.
Hannah: Civil: choose first. Criminal: submit and carry on.
Will: Right: civil normally forces the choice, criminal doesn't. Now questioning witnesses. A leading question suggests the answer wanted or assumes a disputed fact, like "The car was speeding, wasn't it?" A non-leading question is open: "What did you see?"
Hannah: So what's the rule?
Will: Starred. Your own witness, in chief or in re-examination, on a disputed matter: no leading questions. They're allowed on introductory or undisputed matters. In cross-examination, leading questions are permitted and usual.
Hannah: And re-examination?
Will: Only matters arising out of cross-examination. A new matter needs permission.
Hannah: Anything the cross-examiner has to do?
Will: You must put your case on every material point you dispute. Unchallenged evidence may be treated as accepted. That's Browne v Dunn, and the rule is flexible, not rigid. So an opponent's account that's disputed but not challenged: challenge it in cross-examination, not first in closing. The court's control over evidence applies here too: it may exclude admissible evidence and limit cross-examination.
Hannah: What if my own witness turns against me?
Will: A merely unfavourable own witness: you may not cross-examine them. A witness ruled hostile is different. Starred. That's one unwilling to tell the truth for the party calling them. With permission, you may ask leading questions and put a previous inconsistent statement. In civil proceedings that statement is admissible as evidence of its truth.
Hannah: Go on, test me.
Will: Try this one. Dani is a claimant's witness. Her served statement says the defendant's forklift knocked over the racking. In the box she says she "can't really say", and won't look at the claimant's barrister. The barrister wants to read her statement to her and ask, "The forklift knocked over the racking, didn't it?"
Hannah: So can the barrister do that? Yes, as ordinary cross-examination because she's turned awkward; no, never, because she's their own witness; or yes, but only on a hostile ruling and with permission?
Hannah: <think>
Hannah (thinking aloud): This is questioning witnesses. She's the barrister's own witness, so it can't be ordinary cross-examination. A leading question on a disputed matter in chief is out. A merely unfavourable witness can't be cross-examined. But a witness ruled hostile can be led, with permission. So the third.
Will: Right, and the deciding fact is whether she is unwilling to tell the truth for the claimant, or simply unhelpful. If she's merely unfavourable, counsel may not cross-examine her at all. If the judge rules her hostile and gives permission, counsel may ask leading questions and put the statement. In civil proceedings the statement is then admissible as evidence of its truth.
Hannah: Anything left on the courtroom side?
Will: Last part of this topic: manners and duties. Etiquette at a formal hearing: stand when the judge enters or leaves and when addressing the court, and don't interrupt the judge or a witness. Opposing counsel is 'my learned friend'. An opposing solicitor is 'my friend'.
Hannah: Are hearings public?
Will: As a rule, yes. Private only if it's necessary for justice and a listed ground applies. Four to know. One, publicity would defeat the object. Two, confidential information. Three, protecting a child or protected party. Four, national security. Embarrassment alone is not enough.
Hannah: Duties of an advocate?
Will: Four. One, don't mislead the court. Two, make only properly arguable submissions. Three, don't influence witnesses' evidence. Four, don't waste the court's time.
Hannah: What if the law's against me?
Will: Then two starred ones. The advocate must draw the court's attention to relevant cases, statutory provisions and procedural irregularities likely to affect the outcome, even if adverse. So if you know of an adverse Court of Appeal decision the opponent has missed, you cite it.
Hannah (groaning): Even though it hurts.
Will: Yes, even though it hurts. Second, a client admits evidence they gave is false. You mustn't continue in a way that misleads. Without the client's consent to correct it, you cannot disclose the admission, and you must cease to act.
Hannah: And a witness mid-evidence?
Will: A witness part-way through their evidence, including over an adjournment, must not discuss it with anyone, their own lawyers included, until it's finished. The advocate must not confer with them about it.
Hannah: So at lunch the client-witness asks how to answer tomorrow's question, and the answer is no.
Will: Yes: the advocate must not confer with a witness about their evidence while they're part-way through it.
Hannah (summing up): Summonses and trial: a summons compels and a statement doesn't; issue is permission, service is binding; the claimant speaks last; and no leading your own witness on disputed points.
Will: Right: a summons compels, the claimant speaks last, and no leading your own witness on what's disputed. That's evidence of fact. The experts come next, in their own episode.
## Recap
Hannah: All five, in one go?
Will: Evidence must be relevant, with opinion only from experts or as perceived fact; statements stand as evidence in chief; the witness signs, and affidavits are needed only where required; hearsay is admitted and weighed; summonses compel attendance; and the claimant speaks last at trial.
