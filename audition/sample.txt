map:
- Remoteness of damage
- Consent (volenti non fit injuria)
- Contributory negligence

- Remedies and the aim of damages
- Personal injury damages: heads and calculation
- Deductions from damages

recap:
- Remoteness: D is liable for the foreseeable kind of harm, however it happened and however bad it was.
- Consent: a complete defence, but it normally fails for an employee who merely knew of the danger, a rescuer under a duty and the very act, and always for a car passenger.
- Contributory negligence: a partial defence, never 100 per cent, with fixed figures for seatbelts and helmets.
- Illegality: a complete defence; an incidental crime does not bar the claim, but injury that is a foreseeable risk of a joint criminal enterprise is barred.
- Remedies: restore the pre-tort position, normally as one lump sum, and C must mitigate.
- Personal injury damages: special and general damages, Ogden multipliers, and no recovery for a defendant's unpaid care.
- Deductions: benefits come off like for like and never off PSLA; own accident insurance, ill-health or disability pensions and charitable payments or gifts stay.
- Claims on death: the estate, the dependants, and a fixed bereavement award for a few people only.
summary: Remoteness asks whether the kind of damage was foreseeable; then come the defences of consent, contributory negligence and illegality; then remedies and the aim of damages, personal injury damages, deductions from damages, and finally claims on death.

## Map
Will: Right. Eight topics today, and they come in a sensible order. First, how far a defendant's liability stretches, which is remoteness of damage. Then three defences: consent, contributory negligence and illegality.
Hannah: So remoteness, and then three ways the defendant can fight back.
Will: Then the money side. Remedies and the aim of damages, personal injury damages, deductions from damages, and claims on death.
Hannah (thoughtful): Remoteness, three defences, then four topics on what the claimant actually gets. Fine, go.
## Block 1: Remoteness of damage
Will: Remoteness of damage first. The point of it is that a defendant should only pay for the kind of harm they could foresee, not for every direct consequence of what they did. And you ask it after factual causation, never instead of it.
Hannah: So causation gets C in the door, and remoteness decides how far down the corridor they walk.
Will (pleased): Nicely put. The test is whether damage of the relevant kind or type was reasonably foreseeable, judged objectively at the time of breach.
Hannah: So no hindsight.
Will: Right. And if those are met, D is liable even though the way it happened, or the extent of it, was unforeseeable.
Hannah (sceptical): Hang on. How is that different from liability for every direct consequence?
Will: That used to be the rule, and the Wagon Mound rejected it. That's the trap here: it's the foreseeable kind, not all direct consequences. In the Wagon Mound, fire damage from spilled oil was unforeseeable, although pollution damage was foreseeable. So the fire wasn't recoverable.
Hannah: Okay. And what about the way it happens?
Will: Here's the starred point. Someone is burned. Burns were a foreseeable kind of harm, but the burn came from an explosion nobody could have predicted. Is D liable?
Hannah (wary): The explosion wasn't foreseeable, so... no?
Will: Other way. D is liable. Burns were the foreseeable kind of harm, and the unforeseeable way it happened doesn't help D.
Hannah: Right. So an unforeseeable route to a foreseeable kind of harm still counts.
Will: Yes, that's it. Same idea with the injury caused when boys propped up an abandoned boat. The broad risk of children meddling was foreseeable, so the council was liable.
Hannah: So the court looks at the risk broadly.
Will: Usually, yes. Courts usually take a broad view of the type. A foreseeable cold injury covered frostbite, for example. A narrow view is occasionally taken.
Hannah: Occasionally. Go on, show me one.
Will: A splash was foreseeable, but the harm came from an eruption. The court treated an eruption as a different kind of harm from a splash, so there was no liability. That decision is widely criticised.
Hannah (teasing): So the rule isn't "always broad", it's "usually broad".
Will: Which is why the pair matters. In the first story, burns were foreseeable and C is burned by an unforeseeable explosion, so D is liable. In the second, a splash was foreseeable and C is hurt by an eruption, so a different kind of harm and D is not liable. What decides it is how broadly you describe the kind of harm, and usually you describe it broadly.
Hannah: Burns and explosion: liable. Splash and eruption: not liable. Broad description wins, usually.
Will: Yes, that's right.
Will: One more rule on kind of harm, for primary victims. If physical injury was foreseeable, psychiatric injury is recoverable too, because both are personal injury.
Hannah: Same type, so same answer.
Will: Yes. Now the look-alike. Kind of harm is one thing; extent is another. Eggshell skull: D is liable for all the harm, even death, where C's pre-existing condition made it worse.
Hannah: Even if D couldn't know C was fragile.
Will: Yes. The eggshell rule also reaches C's impecuniosity, so extra loss caused by C's lack of money is recoverable. Picture a driver who mounts the pavement and narrowly misses a woman without touching her. She has a history of anxiety and develops severe, long-lasting depression. She can recover for the full psychiatric illness: she was a primary victim, physical injury was foreseeable, so psychiatric harm is the same type, and her vulnerability goes only to extent.
Hannah (brisk): So kind decides whether you're in, and extent doesn't get D out.
Will: Remoteness in one line, then. Foreseeable kind of harm and D is liable, however it came about and however bad it was.
## Block 2: Consent (volenti non fit injuria)
Will: Now from remoteness to the first defence: consent, volenti non fit injuria. The idea is that C freely chose to run a risk, and it defeats the claim entirely. Because of that, courts prefer contributory negligence where both are arguable.
Hannah: All or nothing, so judges reach for the softer option if they can.
Will: Right. Four elements, you need all four, and D has to prove it on the balance of probabilities. First, C had capacity to consent. Second, full knowledge of the risk's nature and extent.
Hannah: Full knowledge. How full is full?
Will: Of the risk's nature and extent. General awareness is not enough. And it's judged subjectively: what did this C actually know?
Hannah: Not what a reasonable person would have known.
Will: Right. That's the trap. The objective standard belongs to contributory negligence, not here. Third element, C agreed, expressly or impliedly, to bear the risk of injury. Fourth, C agreed freely and voluntarily.
Hannah: Capacity, full knowledge, agreement, free choice. And all four or nothing.
Will: Yes. Two small points on how they work. C's own drunkenness counts: a C too drunk to grasp the risk lacks full knowledge. And agreement may be implied from taking part in an obviously dangerous activity. Sports players accept the risks inherent in the sport.
Hannah (brisk): Drunk means no full knowledge; sport means implied agreement to the inherent risks.
Will: Yes. Now the big part. Even with all four elements, volenti normally fails in four situations.
Hannah (groaning): Four exceptions. Go.
Will: An employee who merely knew of the danger. A rescuer acting under a duty. The very act D had a duty to prevent. And a car passenger, where motor insurance is compulsory.
Hannah: Let me take the first. An employee who knew the machine was dangerous and carried on. Surely he accepted the risk.
Will: That's the starred point, and it's the tempting one. Knowledge is not consent. An employee aware of the danger has not thereby agreed to it. Volenti rarely succeeds against employees.
Hannah: Right. Knowing isn't agreeing.
Will: Yes. There is one rare exception: employees who deliberately broke safety rules and regulations together. There, volenti protected the employer from vicarious liability.
Hannah: So it takes deliberate rule-breaking, together.
Will: Yes. Second, the rescuer. A rescuer acting under a legal, social or moral duty doesn't act freely, so volenti normally fails.
Hannah: It's the free choice element that goes. They had to help.
Will: Right. Third, where D had a duty to prevent the very act. The example is suicide in custody. Volenti fails, though contributory negligence may still reduce the damages.
Hannah: Why does volenti fail there? Was it a capacity problem?
Will: No, and this is a look-alike worth fixing. People say the prisoner lacked capacity. In fact he was of sound mind. Volenti failed because suicide was the very act the police had to prevent.
Hannah: Sound mind, but the very act. Capacity wasn't the reason.
Will: Yes, that's right. Fourth, the car passenger, also starred. Any agreement to accept the risk is ineffective where compulsory motor insurance is required. You use contributory negligence instead. This comes from the Road Traffic Act.
Hannah: So a passenger who got in with a drunk driver can't be hit with volenti.
Will: That's it. And the other side of it: a passenger who flew with a pilot he knew was very drunk. There volenti succeeded, because that Road Traffic Act protection covers motor vehicles only.
Hannah: Pilot, volenti succeeds. Car driver, it doesn't. So the pair is about the insurance.
Will: Yes. In the first story, C flies with a pilot he knows is very drunk, so volenti succeeds. In the second, C rides with a car driver she knows is drunk. Volenti is ineffective and damages are reduced instead. What decides it is whether compulsory motor insurance applies.
Hannah (wry): So drunk pilot, defence works; drunk driver, it doesn't. Same drunk, different insurance.
Will: Last piece: exclusion notices. A business cannot exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence, by a term or a notice. That comes from the Unfair Contract Terms Act and the Consumer Rights Act.
Hannah: There's a catch, isn't there?
Will: One. A business occupier may do so towards entrants admitted for recreational or educational purposes outside its business purposes, and hurt by the dangerous state of the premises.
Hannah: Narrow.
Will: Very. Next: knowing of or agreeing to such a notice is not by itself voluntary acceptance of the risk. So a notice doesn't turn into volenti.
Hannah: And for other loss or damage?
Will: There a business exclusion is valid only if it is reasonable.
Will: So take a go-kart track. A customer pays and signs a form saying the track accepts no liability for any injury, and then a kart the track negligently maintained crashes and injures her. The track can't rely on the form as an exclusion, because it's business negligence causing personal injury, and it can't rely on it as consent, because signing a notice is not by itself voluntary acceptance of the risk.
Hannah: Consent in one line: complete defence, four elements, and it normally fails for an employee who merely knew of the danger, a rescuer under a duty and the very act, and always for a car passenger.
Will: Yes, that's it.
## Block 3: Contributory negligence
Will: Now from consent to contributory negligence. The point of it is that C's own carelessness reduces damages but never defeats the claim, which is why courts prefer it to volenti or illegality.
Hannah: The softer option. Partial, not total.
Will: Yes. The test has three steps. First, D proves, on the balance of probabilities, that C failed to take reasonable care for their own safety, and that's judged objectively.
Hannah: That's the objective standard we said belongs here and not in volenti.
Will: Right. Second, that failure contributed to the damage, either by causing the accident or only by worsening the injury.
Hannah: So it doesn't have to cause the crash.
Will: No. Third, damages are reduced by what is just and equitable for C's share in the responsibility. The court weighs causative potency and blameworthiness in setting that share.
Hannah (brisk): Careless, contributed, reduced by what's just and equitable.
Will: Yes. And it is a partial defence: the reduction is always less than 100 per cent, so the claim is never extinguished. That's the trap. It isn't a complete defence like volenti and illegality.
Hannah: Three allowances on the first step, I'm guessing.
Will: Three: agony of the moment, rescuers and children. We'll take them in a minute. First, the starred figures for seatbelts and helmets. C wasn't wearing one. If the injury would have been avoided altogether, 25 per cent off. If it would have been less severe, 15 per cent. If it would have made no difference, nil.
Hannah: Twenty-five, fifteen, nil. Which one is the "less severe" one?
Will: Fifteen. And the fault has to relate to the damage. No helmet but only a leg injury means no reduction.
Hannah: Because the helmet wouldn't have helped a leg.
Will: Yes. Another starred point: a passenger who accepts a lift from a driver she knows is drunk has her damages reduced. Her claim is not defeated.
Hannah: Which fits the volenti exception we just did. Car passenger, so volenti is out and contributory negligence is in.
Will: Right.
Will: Now the allowances. Starred first: a reasonable but mistaken step taken in the agony of the moment attracts no reduction.
Hannah: Fair. You can't judge a split-second decision at leisure.
Will: Second, a rescuer is contributorily negligent only for a wholly unreasonable disregard of their own safety.
Hannah: So the bar for reducing a rescuer's damages is very high.
Will: Right. Third, a child is judged against an ordinarily prudent child of the same age. Very young children are rarely found negligent.
Hannah: Standard follows age.
Will: Yes. One more, and it's a look-alike for what counts as relevant conduct. A prisoner's long-past drug addiction was too remote a cause of his injury to count as contributory negligence. The mistake is thinking the nature of the duty excuses the addicted prisoner. The law is that the addiction was simply too remote a cause.
Hannah: So remote, not excused.
Will: Yes, that's right.
Will: Now later conduct, which is starred. Mere carelessness only reduces damages. Only C's entirely unreasonable later conduct breaks the chain of causation.
Hannah: So a foolish thing C does afterwards just means a reduction.
Will: Right, unless it's entirely unreasonable, and then it breaks the chain.
Will: Two more points. With two defendants, you weigh C's share against the defendants' combined fault, then apportion the rest between them. And contribution between tortfeasors can be a complete indemnity, whereas a contributory negligence reduction never reaches 100 per cent.
Hannah: Indemnity can be total, contributory negligence can't.
Will: Yes, that's the line. Now a scenario. Chloe is a car passenger who isn't wearing her seatbelt. She is hurt in a collision caused equally by two drivers, Ravi and Tom. A belt would have made her injuries less severe. Her full damages are £100,000.
Hannah (thinking aloud): So what does Chloe recover, and how is liability shared? Does she get everything, lose 15 per cent, or lose 25 per cent, and then how do Ravi and Tom split what's left?
Hannah: <think>
Hannah: Right. It's contributory negligence, no seatbelt. The belt would have made her injuries less severe, not avoided them altogether, so it's the 15 per cent figure. Fifteen per cent off £100,000 leaves £85,000. And the drivers are equally at fault, so they bear half each of that.
Will: Yes, £85,000, and Ravi and Tom bear half each. The deciding fact is that her failure only worsened the injury, so it's 15 per cent, not 25. The 15 per cent comes off against the drivers' combined fault, and the remainder is apportioned between them.
Hannah: Contributory negligence in one line: partial, never 100 per cent, and fixed figures for seatbelts and helmets.
Will: Yes.
## Block 4: Illegality
Will: Now from contributory negligence to illegality. The idea is that courts refuse a claim where allowing it would harm the integrity of the legal system. A merely incidental crime does not bar it.
Hannah: So it's not "did C do something naughty".
Will: No. The test comes from Patel v Mirza. Step one: did C commit an illegal, or grossly immoral, act connected with the loss? If not, the defence fails.
Hannah: And if so?
Will: Step two: would allowing the claim create inconsistency in the law? You weigh three points. The underlying purpose of the prohibition, and whether denying the claim enhances it. Any other public policy that denying the claim may undermine.
Hannah: And the third?
Will: Whether denying the claim is proportionate to the illegality.
Hannah: Purpose, other policy, proportionality.
Will: Yes. Proportionality has factors. Seriousness of the conduct, its centrality to the tort, intention, and any marked disparity in culpability. That's four, and the test applies in tort.
Hannah: And this one's a complete defence, isn't it. Not like contributory negligence.
Will: Yes. The claim fails entirely, with no apportionment, and D proves it on the balance of probabilities.
Hannah (sceptical): Then the safe answer is: any crime at all, no recovery.
Will: That's too broad. An incidental crime does not bar the claim, and the Patel v Mirza balance applies.
Hannah: So the crime has to matter to the loss, not just exist.
Will: Right, the crime has to be connected to the loss.
Hannah: So when does a crime bar it?
Will: First, loss flowing from C's own criminal act or sentence is barred. That covers detention, lost earnings and inheritance, even if D's negligence caused C's illness.
Hannah: So the loss comes from the crime or the sentence, not from D.
Will: Right. Second, the starred point: a merely incidental crime does not bar the claim where the negligent driving, not the crime, caused the injury.
Hannah: And the opposite?
Will: Joint criminal enterprise. C cannot recover for injury that is a foreseeable risk of the enterprise, because the injury is treated as caused by C's own crime.
Will: Here's the pair. First story: C is transporting cannabis, D drives negligently and crashes. The crime is incidental, so the claim succeeds. Second: C is a pillion passenger who urges a drunk, unlicensed D to ride recklessly, and they crash. That's a foreseeable risk of the joint crime, so the claim is barred. What decides it is whether the injury came from ordinary negligence or from a risk the crime itself created.
Hannah: Cannabis in the boot and an ordinary bad driver: claim succeeds. Urging on a drunk rider: barred.
Will: Yes. Same thing if two people are driving at normal speed to sell stolen goods hidden in the boot, and the driver negligently misjudges a bend. The passenger's claim can succeed, because the crash came from ordinary negligent driving and the crime was incidental to the injury, not its cause.
Hannah: Illegality in one line: complete defence, but an incidental crime doesn't bar the claim, while injury that is a foreseeable risk of a joint criminal enterprise is barred.
Will: Yes.
## Block 5: Remedies and the aim of damages
Will: Now from the defences to the money, starting with remedies and the aim of damages. Tort damages restore the pre-tort position, normally as one lump sum assessed once and for all.
Hannah: The aim being?
Will: Put C where they would have been had the tort not been committed.
Hannah (wary): That sounds like contract damages.
Will: It's the trap. Not the contract measure. Tort damages restore the pre-tort position, not the position had a promise been performed.
Hannah: Backwards-looking, not promise-looking.
Will: Yes. And C must take reasonable steps to mitigate loss. Loss C could reasonably have avoided is not recoverable.
Hannah: Remedies in one line: back to where they were, normally once and for all, and C has to mitigate.
Will: Yes.
## Block 6: Personal injury damages: heads and calculation
Will: Now from remedies to personal injury damages, heads and calculation. Past financial loss is calculated precisely; PSLA and future loss are assessed.
Hannah: Special damages and general damages, I assume.
Will: Yes. Special damages are past financial losses up to trial, precisely calculable. General damages are pain, suffering and loss of amenity, which is PSLA, plus future losses.
Hannah: How do you value PSLA, though? There's no price tag on pain.
Will: The Judicial College Guidelines and comparable awards, plus a 10 per cent uplift for judgments from 1 April 2013. Loss of amenity reflects how active C was and what C can no longer do.
Hannah: So a keen runner loses more amenity than someone who never ran.
Will: That's the idea. Now the starred one: a permanently unconscious C recovers loss of amenity, assessed objectively, but not pain and suffering.
Hannah: So amenity yes, pain no.
Will: Yes.
Will: Future loss next. For a continuing loss you take the multiplicand, which is the net annual loss, times a multiplier from the Ogden Tables. A one-off future expense, such as adapting a home, is just its cost as a lump sum.
Hannah: So annual loss times the years the loss lasts?
Will: That's the look-alike. You multiply by an Ogden multiplier, which is discounted for early receipt, not by the plain number of years.
Hannah: Ogden, discounted. Not years.
Will: Right. The discount rate from 11 January 2025 is plus 0.5 per cent, previously minus 0.25 per cent. Lost earnings are recovered net of income tax and national insurance.
Hannah: Net, so after tax.
Will: Yes. Next, starred: a living C whose life is shortened recovers lost years earnings, less their own living expenses.
Hannah: Less their own living expenses. Okay.
Will: Right. And loss of earning capacity: a lump sum for C's disadvantage on the labour market if C later loses their job.
Hannah: So it's about being worse placed if you're out of work later.
Will: Yes.
Will: Now care and medical costs, in three bits. Reasonable private medical costs are recoverable, and the availability of NHS treatment is disregarded. Unpaid care by a relative: C recovers its reasonable value, holds it on trust for the carer, and the commercial rate is usually discounted.
Hannah: And the starred point?
Will: Where the unpaid carer is the defendant, nothing is recoverable for that care. So picture a man injured by his wife's negligent driving, and afterwards both his wife and his mother care for him unpaid. He recovers the value of his mother's care only, held on trust, because where the carer is the tortfeasor she would be paying herself.
Hannah: Mother yes, wife no.
Will: Last, the form of the award. Provisional damages are available where there is a chance of later serious disease or serious deterioration, and C may return once if it happens.
Hannah: Once only.
Will: Yes, once. Periodical payments can be ordered without consent only for future pecuniary loss, and only if continuity is reasonably secure.
Hannah: Personal injury damages in one line: special and general damages, Ogden multipliers for future loss, and nothing for a defendant's unpaid care.
Will: Yes.
## Block 7: Deductions from damages
Will: Now from calculating damages to deductions. The basic idea is that state benefits come off only the matching head of loss, and other payments depend on their source.
Hannah: So it depends what the money was for.
Will: Right. Before paying, the compensator repays recoverable benefits to the state through the Compensation Recovery Unit. Benefits are recoverable for 5 years from the day after the accident, or until an earlier final settlement.
Hannah: And a disease, where there's no single day of the accident?
Will: For a disease, the 5 years run from the first claim for a listed benefit because of it.
Hannah: Fine. What comes off what?
Will: Starred. Benefits are offset only like for like, against earnings, care and mobility, and never against PSLA.
Hannah: Never PSLA.
Will: Never. Now payments that are deducted: the employer's contractual sick pay, and a redundancy payment where the injury caused the redundancy.
Hannah: And not deducted?
Will: Also starred. C's own accident insurance, ill-health or disability pensions, and charitable payments or gifts.
Hannah: Contractual sick pay comes off; C's own insurance doesn't.
Will: Yes. Now a scenario. Mina is awarded £40,000 for PSLA and £20,000 for lost earnings. She received £25,000 in recoverable benefits, all paid for lost earnings.
Hannah (brisk): How much does Mina herself receive? Does she get everything, does she lose the benefits from her total, or do they come off the earnings head only?
Hannah: <think>
Hannah: Like for like. The benefits were all for lost earnings, so they only come off the earnings head. That head is £20,000, the benefits are £25,000, so it's reduced to nil. And PSLA can never be touched. So she gets the £40,000.
Will: Yes, £40,000. The deciding fact is like for like: the benefits were all paid for lost earnings, so they reduce only the earnings head, to nil, and PSLA is never touched.
Hannah: Deductions in one line: benefits come off like for like and never off PSLA, and her own accident insurance, ill-health or disability pensions and charitable payments or gifts stay.
Will: Yes.
## Block 8: Claims on death (estate and dependants)
Will: And finally, claims on death, estate and dependants. The estate recovers losses up to death, listed dependants recover lost support, and bereavement is a separate fixed award.
Hannah: Three different claims. Estate, dependants, bereavement.
Will: Right. The estate first. It recovers the deceased's losses up to death, which means PSLA, earnings and expenses, plus funeral expenses.
Hannah: And what does it not get?
Will: No lost-years income, no exemplary damages, no bereavement award, and nothing for death itself.
Hannah: Nothing for death itself. Grim, but clear.
Will: Next, who can claim as a dependant. There are three conditions, and they all have to be met. First, the deceased could have sued D for the injury had they survived. Second, the claimant is on the statutory list of dependants.
Hannah: And the third?
Will: The claimant lost a reasonable expectation of financial benefit, meaning money or services.
Hannah: So does the dependant have to have been financially dependent already?
Will: That's the look-alike, and the answer is no. The law is a reasonable expectation of financial benefit, and even a future one suffices.
Hannah: So an expected benefit counts, not just a current one.
Will: Yes, that's right. How it works: the personal representatives sue for all dependants. A dependant may sue if the personal representatives have not done so within 6 months. And only one action is allowed.
Hannah: One action, and the personal representatives go first.
Will: Right. And a starred point: there is no claim if the deceased settled before death, or their claim was already time-barred.
Hannah: Because the first condition fails. They couldn't have sued.
Will: Yes.
Will: Now the list. Starred, the partners who qualify: a spouse or civil partner, a former spouse or civil partner, and a cohabitant of 2 years immediately before death.
Hannah: Two years, right up to the death.
Will: Yes. Other dependants, in two groups. A parent or ascendant, a person treated as a parent, and a child or descendant.
Hannah: And the rest?
Will: A child of the family, and a sibling, uncle or aunt, or their issue. Relationships by marriage count as blood, half blood as whole blood, and a stepchild as a child.
Hannah: What about a close friend who relied on the deceased?
Will: No. Relationship alone is not enough, and nor is dependence by someone off the list, such as a friend. But an expected future benefit suffices.
Will: Put it together. A man is negligently killed. He had lived with his partner for 18 months, and he sent his adult half-brother money every month. Only the half-brother has a dependency claim: half blood counts as whole blood and he expected financial benefit, whereas the partner falls short of 2 years' cohabitation immediately before death.
Hannah: So the partner is out on the 2 years, and the half-brother is in.
Will: Dependency damages next. The dependency is a multiplicand, the annual lost dependency, times a multiplier. For a spouse, the conventional starting point is two-thirds of the deceased's net income with no children, and three-quarters with dependent children.
Hannah: Is that a rule?
Will: No. The fractions are a conventional starting point, not a fixed rule. And the multiplier for future loss runs from the date of trial, not the date of death.
Hannah: Trial, not death. Okay.
Will: Now what's ignored. Starred: benefits resulting from the death, such as an inheritance or life insurance, are disregarded. A widow's remarriage, or her prospects of remarriage, are disregarded too.
Hannah: So an inheritance doesn't reduce the dependency.
Will: Right. For a cohabitant, the court considers that they had no enforceable right to financial support. The deceased's contributory negligence reduces the dependants' damages proportionately. And dependants may also recover funeral expenses that they paid.
Will: Last, bereavement. Starred: bereavement damages go to a spouse or civil partner, to a 2-year cohabitant, or to the parents of a minor who was never married or civilly partnered. If the child was illegitimate, the mother alone.
Hannah: And not to children.
Will: Also starred. No bereavement award to the deceased's children, or to parents of an adult child, though they may still claim dependency.
Will: The fixed sum is £15,120 for deaths on or after 1 May 2020, divided equally where two people are entitled.
Hannah: Fixed, and shared if there are two.
Will: Yes. Now a scenario. Jack is aged 17 and unmarried, and he's killed by D's negligence. He was 20 per cent to blame. His married parents are both alive.
Hannah (brisk): So what bereavement damages does each parent receive? The full £15,120 each, half of it each, or half of it each reduced by the 20 per cent?
Hannah: <think>
Hannah: Jack is a minor who never married, so his parents are the ones entitled. Two people, so the fixed sum is divided equally. Then the 20 per cent contributory negligence comes off. So half each, less 20 per cent, which is £6,048 each.
Will: Yes, £6,048 each. The deciding fact is that parents of an unmarried minor share the fixed £15,120 equally, and Jack's 20 per cent contributory negligence reduces the damages under the Act.
Hannah: Claims on death in one line: the estate, the dependants, and a fixed bereavement award for a few people only.
Will: Yes.
## Recap
Hannah: Right, so that's the whole run, from remoteness through the defences, then the money.
Will: Remoteness asks whether the kind of damage was foreseeable; then come the defences of consent, contributory negligence and illegality; then remedies and the aim of damages, personal injury damages, deductions from damages, and finally claims on death.
